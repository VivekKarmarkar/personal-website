1The Doctor's Hands

Close your eyes and imagine a doctor examining a patient. They press gently on the abdomen, feeling for something that shouldn't be there. This is palpation—one of the oldest diagnostic techniques in medicine, practiced for over 3,000 years.

What are they feeling for? Stiffness. A tumor is like a hidden pebble in a bowl of jelly—when you press on the surface, the stiff spot reveals itself through how the surrounding tissue deforms.

🍮

The Jello Analogy

Imagine a bowl of Jello with a marble hidden inside. When you poke the surface, the Jello near the marble doesn't jiggle the same way as the rest. An experienced chef (or doctor) can feel where the marble is just by how the surface responds to touch. That's exactly what our AI learns to do.

But here's the challenge: what if you could only feel the edges of the bowl, not poke anywhere you want? What if you had to figure out where the marble is using only boundary measurements?

That's the puzzle we're solving.

2Learning from CT Scans

In 1971, Godfrey Hounsfield did something remarkable. He took X-ray images from many different angles around a patient and used mathematics to reconstruct what was inside. This became the CT scan, and it won him a Nobel Prize.

"What if we could do for touch what CT did for X-rays?"

We borrowed this insight. Instead of taking one measurement, we press on the tissue from many different angles, like a CT scanner rotating around a patient. Each press gives us a piece of the puzzle. Combine them all, and suddenly the hidden structure becomes visible.

3The AI That Checks Its Work

Here's where the AI magic comes in. We train a neural network—think of it as a very eager student—to guess what the stiffness map looks like inside the tissue.

But here's the clever part: we don't just accept the guess. We check it.

Step 1: The Student Guesses

The AI looks at how the surface deformed and guesses: "I think there's a stiff blob right here."

Step 2: Physics Checks the Homework

We use physics simulation to ask: "If that guess were true, how would the surface actually deform?"

Step 3: Compare & Learn

If the simulated deformation doesn't match reality, the AI learns from its mistake and tries again.

Step 4: Repeat Until Perfect

After thousands of iterations, the AI becomes remarkably good at guessing correctly.

📝

The Math Test Analogy

Imagine a student learning to solve word problems. They don't just memorize answers—they guess a solution, plug it back into the original problem to check if it works, and adjust if it doesn't. Our AI does exactly this, using physics as its "answer key." It's learning physics by doing physics.

Why Should You Care?

Current medical imaging for tissue stiffness requires expensive equipment:

  • MRI-based elastography: $1-3 million machines
  • Ultrasound elastography: $50,000-200,000 systems
  • PAT Scan vision: Potentially just sensors on a flexible band

Imagine a world where early tumor detection doesn't require a hospital visit. Where a wearable device could monitor tissue changes over time. Where healthcare reaches places that have never had access to advanced imaging.

💡 The Big Idea

We're teaching AI the ancient skill of palpation—the doctor's intuition encoded into algorithms. By combining the wisdom of physics with the pattern-recognition power of neural networks, we can make the invisible visible using nothing but touch.

4Forward vs. Inverse Problems

Now let's get more precise about what we just described. At the heart of PAT Scan lies a fundamental distinction in physics: the difference between forward problems (easy) and inverse problems (hard).

✅ Forward Problem

Given: Material properties + Applied forces
Find: Resulting deformation

"If I know what the tissue is made of and how hard I push, what shape does it become?"

❌ Inverse Problem

Given: Observed deformation + Applied forces
Find: Material properties

"Given how the tissue deformed when I pushed, what must it be made of?"

The forward problem has a unique, stable solution—you can solve it with standard numerical methods. The inverse problem is ill-posed: multiple different material distributions could produce similar boundary measurements.

🎯 The Core Insight

This is precisely what we described in the "student checking homework" analogy! Instead of trying to directly solve the ill-posed inverse problem, we train a neural network to propose material distributions (the guess), then use the well-posed forward problem to check if the proposal matches observations (physics checking homework). The network learns to make better proposals through gradient descent.

5The FEM Equation

To simulate how tissue deforms (the "physics checking homework" step), we discretize the continuous material into a mesh of triangular elements. Each element has its own material properties (Young's modulus E, Poisson's ratio ν).

The Fundamental Equation

All of linear elasticity boils down to one elegant equation:

Linear Elasticity (FEM Form)
F = K · U
Force vector = Stiffness matrix × Displacement vector

Let's unpack this:

  • F — Applied forces at each node (what we control)
  • K — Global stiffness matrix (encodes ALL material properties and geometry)
  • U — Resulting displacements at each node (what we measure at boundaries)

🧱 What's in the Stiffness Matrix K?

The stiffness matrix K is assembled from individual element stiffness matrices, each computed from the element's shape, material properties, and geometric configuration. It's like a giant spring network—K tells you how much each node "resists" when you push on any other node.

Critical insight: K depends on the material properties E(x,y). Change the materials → change K → change how forces produce displacements.

Forward Problem = Linear System Solve

Given K and F, finding U requires solving the linear system K · U = F. We use torch.linalg.solve to find U—this is computationally tractable and well-posed. Modern sparse linear algebra makes this fast even for large meshes.

Step by Step

  1. U-Net Predicts Material Field: Given boundary displacement measurements, the U-Net outputs a 64×64 image representing the predicted Young's modulus E(x,y) across the domain.
  2. Sample at Mesh Nodes: The continuous prediction is sampled at each element centroid to get discrete material values for the FEM mesh.
  3. Build Stiffness Matrix K: Using the predicted materials, we assemble the global stiffness matrix K from individual element contributions.
  4. FEM Forward Solve: Solve F = KU to get predicted displacements U_pred everywhere in the mesh.
  5. Extract Boundary Displacements: Pull out just the boundary node displacements from U_pred—these are what we can actually measure experimentally.
  6. Compare & Backpropagate: Compare predicted boundary displacements to actual measurements. Compute loss and backpropagate through the ENTIRE pipeline (including the FEM solve!) to update U-Net weights.

💡 Why This Works

The network doesn't just learn pattern matching—it learns physics. Every gradient update incorporates information about how material changes affect deformation. The physics simulator acts as an "answer checker" that enforces physical consistency.

7Loss Functions & Regularization

The loss function guides the network toward good solutions. We use a combination of data fidelity and regularization terms.

Total Loss
L = L_data + λ_TV · L_TV
Data fidelity term + Total Variation regularization

Data Fidelity: MSE on Boundary Displacements

Data Loss
L_data = ||U_boundary^pred - U_boundary^meas||²
Squared difference between predicted and measured boundary displacements

This term ensures our predictions match experimental observations. Note that we only compare at the boundary—this reflects real experimental constraints where internal measurements are unavailable.

Regularization: Total Variation (TV)

TV Regularization
L_TV = Σ |∇E(x,y)|
Sum of gradient magnitudes encourages piecewise-constant solutions

Total Variation regularization is borrowed from image processing. It penalizes unnecessary variation in the material field, encouraging piecewise-constant solutions with sharp boundaries—exactly what we expect for tissue with distinct inclusions.

⚖️ The Regularization Trade-off

Too little TV (λ_TV small): Noisy predictions, overfitting to measurement noise
Too much TV (λ_TV large): Over-smoothed predictions, loss of fine details

We use hyperparameter search to find the sweet spot (typically λ_TV ≈ 0.001-0.01).

8Key Innovations

CT-Inspired Multi-Angle Acquisition

Just as CT scans acquire X-ray projections from many angles (remember the Hounsfield insight?), we apply forces from multiple angles around the boundary. This dramatically improves reconstruction quality by providing more independent measurements.

📐 Boussinesq-Inspired Angular Spacing

The optimal angular spacing isn't uniform! We derive it from the Boussinesq solution for a point load on an elastic half-space. The stress field decays as cos²θ from the loading point, which suggests spacing forces according to inverse-cosine intervals for uniform information density.

🧪 Physics-Informed by Design

PAT Scan is a mesh-based Physics-Informed Neural Network (PINN). Unlike meshfree PINNs that embed PDEs directly in the loss function, we leverage established FEM solvers for the physics while using deep learning only for the challenging inverse mapping. This hybrid approach combines the reliability of traditional numerical methods with the flexibility of neural networks.

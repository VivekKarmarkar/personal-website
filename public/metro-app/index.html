Introduction

The situation I had in mind was concrete. I am standing outside a chai shop in Dumbo, Brooklyn, hungry, and I want Hyderabadi food at a restaurant in Manhattan. A New Yorker friend would answer in three sentences: walk to York Street, take the F into Manhattan, change at West 4th. What the friend also does, without being asked, is check that the train is really running that evening, because on any given night one line is rerouted and another ends early. I wanted my coding agent to do the whole thing by voice, from a place name to a verified route, and to say plainly when it could not verify a leg.

This project tests MCP servers discovered through Goose, Block's open-source AI-agent platform, whose demos and extension directory make servers easy to find and try. An MCP server exposes tools to a coding agent. Tools alone do not know how I want decisions made, so a skill, a repeatable set of instructions the agent follows, bridges my intent and what the tools can do. Skills are at the heart of Claude Code OS because they keep those workflows for reuse. For the metro server the path was the usual one: install it globally, inspect its thirteen tools by hand, draw the explainer above, try simple examples, turn my questions and Claude's into a test suite, run it, build the skill from what we learned, add it with /sync-os, and run the finished skill once on the Dumbo case.

01

The tests we ran

We ran fourteen tests against the live server, shaped by the Dumbo question. We searched for the two stations near Dumbo and for hub names such as Times Square and 28th Street to see how the server handles stations that share a name. We pulled the full station list for the F and the A, the plain-English service description for the F, the transfer links at a hub and at an isolated stop, the entire network, a station that does not exist, a bare-number query, live arrivals at a two-line station, the current incident feed, and one call against the Washington DC side of the server.

Then we ran the case for real, on a Tuesday evening: geocode the chai shop and the restaurant, find the nearest stations, enumerate candidate routes, and verify each leg of the best one with eight further calls for line membership, service pattern, incidents, transfer links, and departures.

02

What we learned

The server answers every primitive question well: thirteen of fourteen tests passed, the last was inconclusive when the connection dropped. Station search, line membership, route descriptions, transfers, live arrivals, and incidents all returned real, current data with no credentials. The live incident feed caught two things on our evening that a static map never would: the E was rerouted onto the Sixth Avenue line from West 4th, so it could not reach 50th Street, and the C carried a "service ends early" notice.

The limits are all things the tools leave to the caller. There is no geocoder and no "stations near me" tool, so a place name has to become coordinates elsewhere and the nearest station is a local computation over the full station list. There is no routing tool at all. A line's station list is the union of every branch it ever runs, so membership proves a train can stop somewhere, not that it does tonight. Arrival predictions encode direction as north or south, not as a destination, and most of them are timetable entries rather than live trains. Same-name stations collide: there are four records for Times Square and three separate 23rd Street stations one avenue apart. Incident text often has no times, and planned work from past weeks stays listed. Two responses are large enough to swamp a model's context and have to be filtered locally.

03

Why a skill

Those findings leave a fixed set of questions every time. How does "the chai shop in Dumbo" become a latitude and longitude, and how do we catch the geocoder picking the wrong borough? Which of the nearby stations should count, and which lines do they carry? Given two stations, how do we enumerate a direct route or a one-transfer route, and how do we throw away transfers that first carry you the wrong way? Once a route is proposed, which calls prove each leg: that the line stops at both ends, that the branch runs at this hour, that no incident cuts the path, that the two platforms at the transfer are actually linked? Which direction is Manhattan-bound? And when a check cannot be run, how do we say so instead of calling the route verified? The skill is the repeatable answer to those questions.

04

The skill

/subway-route takes an origin and a destination as place names or addresses and returns a route with every leg checked. It first resolves the current New York time, because the hour decides which branches run. Two small helpers fill the gaps the server leaves: one geocodes each place with a borough check and ranks the nearest stations by walking distance from a cached copy of the station list; the other enumerates direct and one-transfer candidates and ranks transfers by how far they detour from the straight line. The skill then verifies the chosen route with the server's own tools, in order: line membership at origin, transfer, and destination; the service pattern for the hour; the incident feed filtered to the route's lines; the transfer link between platforms; and the next departures from the origin, marked as live or scheduled.

On the Dumbo evening it answered: F northbound from York Street to West 4th, a three-minute transfer to the uptown C, off at 50th Street, four minutes' walk. It stated two caveats, that the C ends early at an unstated time and that the live C check at West 4th was not run, and gave the fallback of the uptown A to 42nd Street with an eleven-minute walk. It also told me what to avoid: High Street, where the A was rerouted, and the E, which was off Eighth Avenue that night.
